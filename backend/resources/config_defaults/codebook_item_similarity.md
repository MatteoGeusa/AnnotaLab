# Codebook — Item Similarity Assessment

## 1. SYNONYMY / ANTONYMY

### Synonymy

**Definition:** Two items are considered synonymous when they possess identical or nearly identical linguistic meaning.

**Examples:**

- "I feel happy" and "I am very content"
- "I am making a decision" and "I am making a choice"
- "I feel sad" and "I am feeling low"
- "I get along well with my colleagues" and "I feel comfortable with the people I work with"

### Antonymy

**Definition:** Two items that express opposite or nearly opposite linguistic meanings.

**Examples:**

- "I am a successful person" and "I am a failure"
- "I tend to sleep well" and "I often have difficulty resting at night"
- "I am a cheerful person" and "I am a person who tends to feel depressed"
- "I deeply love classical music" and "I cannot stand classical music"

### Coder Instructions

Evaluate whether the two items share the same (or nearly the same) meaning, or if they express opposite (or nearly opposite) concepts.

**[Forced choice among three alternatives]**

- Item A is a synonym of Item B
- Item A is the opposite of Item B
- No such relationship exists between Item A and Item B

> **Note:** Many psychometric scales rely on syntactic variations of items that share approximately the same meaning. Conversely, items with opposite meanings are frequently utilized to measure the same underlying psychological construct.

## 2. INCLUSION RELATIONS

### Subset / Hyponymy

**Definition:** The content described in one item is (approximately) a subset of the content described in another item. The specific item may naturally belong to other sets beyond those under evaluation. Such items share a common "scale of abstraction" or theme, but one is more specific than the other. These sets should be interpreted as "fuzzy" or not strictly delimited. Concepts: Set/Subset, General/Specific, Levels of Abstraction, Hierarchical Relations.

**Examples:**

- "I enjoy jogging in the park" (specific, subset) and "Exercising" (general, set)
- "I am an emotional person" and "I am often anxious"
- "Performing mathematics exercises" and "Studying"
- "I am depressed" and "I feel unwell"
- "I play football to become rich and famous" and "I practice a sport"

### Coder Instructions

An object, activity, or action may be more general than another and include it. Determine if such a hierarchy exists between the items.

**[Forced choice among three alternatives]**

- A is more specific and is included within B
- B is more specific and is included within A
- No such relationship exists

> **Note:** Items within a psychometric scale "should" ideally possess a similar level of abstraction. Identifying asymmetries in the levels of abstraction across different contents or items of a construct provides significant scientific insight.

### Common Superset (Common Parent / Co-hyponymy)

**Definition:** Two items, which are neither synonymous nor antonymous, belong to a proximate common superset. This superset is closely related to both items, representing the most immediate general category that includes them. Metaphorically, this relationship mirrors that of siblings: offspring of the same parent. Multiple common supersets may exist; as with other inclusion relations, these categories are considered non-precisely defined or "fuzzy."

**Examples:**

- "I am angry" and "I feel sad" → a common superset is "being in a bad mood" or "experiencing an emotion"
- "I often have breakfast out" and "I often have dinner out" → a common superset is "dining out"
- "I commit to my studies because I enjoy it" and "I commit to my studies to please my parents" → "academic commitment"
- "I often hug my partner" and "I often caress my partner" → "affectionate gestures toward the partner"

### Coder Instructions

Two objects, activities, or actions may belong to a shared common category. Assess whether the items maintain this relationship.

**[Forced choice between two alternatives and a filtered open response]**

- A and B belong to the same, slightly more general category of objects/activities.
  - If yes, specify the category: _(open question)_
- There is no common category for A and B

> **Note:** Scale items are often conceptualized as elements sharing a common superset (the construct). Thus, various items cover different aspects or contents of the construct, which is not explicitly represented by any single item but includes them all. It is critical to distinguish this inclusion relationship from synonymy/antonymy.

## 3. CAUSAL RELATIONS

### Probabilistic Causal Relations

**Definition:** Based on common real-world knowledge, the coder anticipates a causal (probabilistic) relationship with a prevailing direction between the two items. A causal relationship here refers to a connection between two variables, where a change in one variable (the cause) leads directly or indirectly (through intermediate steps) to a change in another variable (the effect). The relationship is understood in probabilistic terms; it does not need to occur in 100% of cases, but rather represents a statistically significant perceived association. The judgment of the causal direction is based on perceived prevalence; inverse relationships or feedback loops are possible but are not the primary focus of this coding.

**Examples:**

- "I have recently lost a loved one" increases the probability of "I am sad"
- "I have had difficulty sleeping lately" increases the probability of "I feel tired lately"
- "I often go to parties" increases the probability of "I often meet new people"
- "I recently received compliments at work" increases the probability of "I have been feeling good about going to work lately"

### Coder Instructions

One occurrence increases the probability of another when the first frequently (though not necessarily always) results in the second. The first event may lead, directly or indirectly, to the other as a consequence. For example, heavy and prolonged smoking frequently leads to pulmonary disease.

**[Forced choice among three alternatives]**
Select the situation you perceive as most frequent.

- A increases the probability of B
- B increases the probability of A
- No such relationship exists between A and B

> **Note:** In psychometrics, items are often viewed as effects of a construct (reflective models), as causes (formative models), or as maintained in direct causal interaction (network models). Although these relationships should be established empirically rather than through subjective perception, the perspective of a potential respondent or an expert remains highly valuable.
